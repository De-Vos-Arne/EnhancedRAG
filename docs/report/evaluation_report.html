Belief-System archive · R0–R8 · blind-rated

Does metadata-enhanced retrieval actually help?

27 questions, 243 answers, one "Overall" score each (0–100), collected blind. Nine regimes span a lexical baseline (R0) up through full metadata fusion (R6), a raw long-context control (R7), and pooled keyword+vector curation (R8).

Headline

Quality climbs almost monotonically from the keyword baseline to the metadata-aware regimes, and every regime from R1 onward beats R0 with a statistically significant margin (paired t-test, 95% CI excludes zero — see results/table.csv). The improvement is not attributable to any single mechanism: weighting, tree-path embedding, highlighted-only filtering, and prompt-visible metadata each add a further increment on their own.

What the data actually shows

Corrected premise

X2 ("quantum computing") did not test what it was designed to test

X2 was written as an out-of-corpus stress test. It is not one: the archive contains a genuine "Quantum computer notes" section — business and technology tangents, not doctrine — inside the in-scope pages. R6 and R8 both named that section directly and scored accordingly low (7, 9), correctly, because the section itself is thin. The more informative result is elsewhere: R2, R4 and R5 all stated confidently that the archive contains no mention of quantum computing — a false negative rather than a graceful handling of a genuine gap. Their weight- and filter-based mechanisms appear to have pruned the tangential section out before generation ever saw it, where R0's plain lexical match and R6/R7/R8's broader retrieval both surfaced it. This indicates that aggressive weight filtering can produce confident, wrong answers, not only silent misses.

Retrieval gap

F3 ("the intellectual spine") has a correct answer that no regime surfaced

Every regime scored F3 between 2 and 16, the lowest ceiling of any question in the set. This is not a case of the archive lacking an answer — the archive states the answer explicitly elsewhere, in older material that predates the author's heavier use of highlighting. The likely cause is salience drift: colour usage intensified over the archive's history, so foundational passages written early carry systematically lower weight than later material saying less. Weight-based retrieval, which favours highlighted text, would underweight exactly this kind of case. This is a limitation of weight-driven retrieval on an archive whose highlighting habits are not stationary over time, not evidence that the content is missing.

Counter-example

C1 is a case where more metadata produced a worse answer

On C1 ("core philosophical influences"), R2/R4/R5 scored 59–61, noted as covering the essence but incomplete, while R6 (24) and R8 (17) scored far lower, noted as containing inaccuracies and material irrelevant to the question. R7 (70, full long-context stuffing) scored highest. This is kept as an explicit counter-example rather than smoothed into the aggregate: on this particular synthesis question, R6 and R8's broader, weight-fused retrieval pulled in material that measurably hurt the answer.

Possible false positive

E2 may reward a lucky retrieval rather than a robust one

On E2 ("greatest threat to civilization"), R2 (80) and R4 (80) score among the highest of any answer in the set, well above their typical performance elsewhere — R2 and R4 average 47 and 49 overall. The rating note on R2 reads "very clever synthesis," but the size of the gap relative to those regimes' usual behaviour is more consistent with a single highly relevant fragment being retrieved by chance than with a systematic capability. Included in the aggregate as rated, but not treated as representative of R2/R4's typical retrieval quality.

Does more context simply mean a better answer?

Not on its own. Fragment counts across the nine regimes cluster into three discrete bands rather than varying continuously — 12 (R0–R5's fixed pool), roughly 18–60 (R6/R8's expansion and pooling), and roughly 230 (R7's long-context stuffing) — so the three bands are compared directly rather than fitting a trend line across a gap with no actual samples in it (figure: results/figures/fig5_context_volume_vs_quality.png, n=243). Median quality rises from the 12-fragment band (~48) to the 18–60 band (~70), but does not rise further at ~230 fragments (~70, statistically indistinguishable from the middle band) — and the ~230-fragment band's own spread is wide, from 14 to 80. R6 and R8 reach the same quality as R7 with a fifth to a quarter of the fragment count, by choosing which fragments rather than how many. This is consistent with the C1 counter-example above, where added volume actively hurt one answer, and argues against "just retrieve more" as a substitute for weighted, structured retrieval.
